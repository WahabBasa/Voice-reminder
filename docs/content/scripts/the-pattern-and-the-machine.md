## COLD OPEN

*(v3 — rewritten for the ear: one idea per sentence, explain before epigram, no
pause engineering — punctuation does the pacing.)*

[VISUAL: a single punched card, rotating slowly against black]

This is a punched card.

It's a piece of cardboard, about the size of your hand. It looks like nothing. But
this piece of cardboard might be the most important object of the last two hundred
years.

Here's why. Every hole in this card is a decision. Hole means yes. No hole means
no. The phone in your pocket makes billions of yes-or-no decisions every second —
and every one of them is a descendant of this card.

This is the story of how a piece of cardboard taught machines to follow
instructions. It runs through three machines. The first is a loom in France, that
weaves silk. The second is a computer in London, that was never built. And the
third is a thirty-ton monster in Philadelphia, built to aim artillery.

Let's start with the loom.

## ACT ONE — THE PATTERN LEAVES THE MACHINE

### ✅ APPROVED OPENING (the quality bar — locked 2026-08-22)

Lyon, France. The early seventeen hundreds.

[VISUAL: a workshop interior, warm light, looms in rows]

Step inside a silk workshop, and the first thing that hits you is the sound.
Dozens of wooden looms, clacking like a room full of typewriters. Silk dust
floating in the light. This is where the finest cloth in Europe gets made —
pictures woven in silk. Flowers. Birds. Whole gardens you can wear. Kings pay
fortunes for it.

And on every loom, two people. A master weaver at the shuttle. Above him, perched
in the frame, a boy, pulling threads one at a time, in the order the master calls
out. Today the boy pulls one thread wrong — and a foot of silk is ruined. It
takes a week to unpick.

This is the deal the whole city lives on: the most valuable cloth on Earth, made
by hand, one pull at a time, stored nowhere except inside the people who make it.

Now follow the silk money as it flows in. It pays the weavers. It pays the
designers, the dyers, the merchants. And when a city fills with that kind of
money, one more trade always arrives: the makers of beautiful, clever, useless
things. Clockmakers. Automaton builders. Organ builders — men who make
instruments that play themselves, for parlors that can afford anything.

[VISUAL: an organ shop; the pinned barrel turning]

Walk past one of their shops and listen. An organ playing with nobody at the
keys. Inside, a slow-turning barrel covered in metal pins, each pin tripping its
note at exactly the right moment. It never misses. It *can't* miss. The song is
nailed down.

So by seventeen twenty-five, Lyon holds the two most intricate machines in
Europe, a street apart. The loom, which makes the money — and cannot remember its
own pattern. The organ, which the money buys — and never forgets its song.

Thousands of families live off the looms. Dozens live off the music machines. Two
trades, packed into the same narrow streets, generation after generation. Sooner
or later they were going to cross — an organ builder's son was going to end up in
silk, with both machines in his hands.

The one it happened to was named Basile Bouchon.

And here's the arithmetic of Bouchon's life. A weaver isn't paid for his hours —
he's paid by the bolt. Finish the cloth, get the money. And out of that money he
pays for everything: his silk, his loom… and the boy. The boy's wages. The boy's
years of training. The boy's mistakes. When a pull goes wrong and a foot of silk
is ruined, the merchant doesn't eat that week. The weaver does.

Now look at the organ again — the way a man like that would look at it. The organ
doesn't tire at hour ten. The organ doesn't need wages, or training, or dinner.
The organ never misses.

Nobody recorded why Bouchon began. But every man in silk could do that
arithmetic. He was just the one standing close enough to the answer.

Nobody recorded his experiments, either. But we know the machine he finished
with — and a finished machine is a list of problems its builder had to solve.

Problem one: something has to *say* which threads rise. He starts with paper —
the cheapest thing in Lyon that can hold a hole. One spot along the row for each
thread. Hole, rise. Blank, stay. He punches a test strip, hangs it on the loom,
weaves. And the first cloth comes out garbled — one hole a hair out of line lifts
the *wrong* thread, and the error repeats down the whole strip. Spacing is
everything. He learns to punch against a guide.

Problem two: a loom works row by row — so the paper must step, exactly one row,
with every throw of the shuttle. Step too far, and the pattern shears sideways as
it weaves, the little figure sliding apart diagonally, like a picture cut and
restacked wrong. So the paper's movement gets tied to the loom's own rhythm.
Throw. Press. Step. Throw. Press. Step.

And then one morning, it holds. A strip of plain cloth with a small figure
repeating down its length — correct, correct, correct, correct — woven by nobody.
The loom read the paper.

The paper is the boy.

It survives a day. Then the paper softens at the pin-holes, snags — and tears.
Paper can carry a song in a parlor. It cannot survive a workshop.

[VISUAL: the woven Jacquard portrait]

And the machine could do things nobody expected. In 1839, weavers produced a
portrait of Jacquard himself — woven in silk, so fine that people mistook it for a
pencil drawing. It took about twenty-four thousand punched cards. Twenty-four
thousand cards of hole-or-no-hole. And the result has a face.

But here's the real revolution, and it's easy to miss. When the cloth is finished,
the weaver doesn't rebuild his loom. He lifts off the chain of cards, and hangs up
a different chain. Same machine. Different cards. Completely different cloth.

The cards are to the loom what a record is to a record player. The player stays.
The music changes.

Think about what that means. The pattern doesn't live inside the machine anymore.
It lives on the cards. And cards can be copied. Carried. Sold. Stolen.

For the first time in history, what a machine *is*, and what a machine *does*, are
two separate things.

Everything else in this story — the computer that was never built, the thirty-ton
monster, the phone in your pocket — is what happens when people take that idea
seriously.

## ACT TWO — THE PATTERN OUTLIVES THE MACHINE

[VISUAL: a London drawing room; the woven portrait on the wall]

A London drawing room, some thirty years later. On the wall hangs one of those
woven portraits. Its owner tests his guests with it — nearly all of them call it an
engraving, and nearly all of them are wrong.

The owner is Charles Babbage, a mathematician designing a machine that resembles
nothing on Earth: the Analytical Engine. It has a "mill," where numbers are worked
on — we would say processor. A "store," where numbers wait — we would say memory.
And in June 1836, staring at the invention hanging on his wall, Babbage decides how
to command it: punched cards.

Two kinds of cards. Operation cards — *what to do*. Variable cards — *what to do
it to*.

Read that again slowly. The instruction and the data, as two separate stacks of
cardboard. In 1836.

[VISUAL: Ada Lovelace portrait]

The person who sees what this truly means is Ada Lovelace — daughter of the poet
Byron, raised on mathematics, one of the few people alive who understands the
Engine at all. In 1843 she publishes the first algorithm ever written for a
general-purpose machine: a step-by-step method for the Engine to compute a sequence
called the Bernoulli numbers. And she sees past arithmetic entirely: the numbers in
the machine, she writes, could stand for anything — symbols, letters, musical
notes. The Engine would not merely calculate. It would compute.

She explains it in the only sentence this story has ever needed:

> "We may say most aptly, that the Analytical Engine weaves algebraical patterns
> just as the Jacquard-loom weaves flowers and leaves."

Now the strangest fact of the century. The Analytical Engine was never built. Not
that year. Not in Babbage's lifetime. Not ever.

Which means the first published program in history was written for a machine that
did not exist — and it was still *correct*.

Think about what that proves. In Lyon, the pattern left the machine. In London, the
pattern no longer even *needs* the machine. You can write cloth for a loom nobody
has made.

The cards, meanwhile, got a job — counting the 1890 American census on a hundred
million of them, electrically, years faster than clerks ever had; the little
company behind it would one day be called IBM. For the next half century, the
intangible thing wore cardboard.

## ACT THREE — THE PATTERN GETS TRAPPED — AND MOVES INSIDE

February 14th, 1946. The Moore School of Electrical Engineering, University of
Pennsylvania. Reporters are being led into a room that hums.

[VISUAL: ENIAC — panels, cables, blinking neons]

They are looking at ENIAC: seventeen thousand, four hundred and sixty-eight vacuum
tubes, five million hand-soldered joints, thirty tons, drawing about a hundred and
fifty kilowatts. Built for the Army to compute artillery firing tables — thousands
of trajectories, each one a differential equation, previously solved by rooms full
of people whose actual job title was "computer."

Six of those human computers are now something that doesn't have a name yet. Kay
McNulty. Betty Jennings. Betty Snyder. Marlyn Wescoff. Fran Bilas. Ruth Lichterman.
There is no manual, no programming language, no such job as "programmer" — they
learn the machine from its wiring diagrams and make it compute.

At the public reveal, ENIAC computes a shell's trajectory in about twenty seconds.
The shell itself takes about thirty to land. The room has just outrun the
artillery.

[VISUAL: hands re-plugging a plugboard, close]

But look closer, because something here has gone wrong — something almost funny.

To give ENIAC a *new* problem, the six women spend days re-plugging hundreds of
cables and resetting thousands of switches. Weeks of planning, days of wiring — for
seconds of computation. The program *is* the wiring.

Do you see it? The most advanced machine on Earth has quietly undone Jacquard. The
pattern has crawled back inside the mechanism. A hundred and forty years after
Lyon, the fastest computer alive is a loom with the flowers nailed in.

The fix is described in 1945, in a report on ENIAC's successor: store the program
*in the machine's memory* — as numbers, side by side with the data it works on.

And that is the moment. Not a bigger machine, not a faster one — a relocation. In
Lyon the pattern lived on cards *outside* the machine. Now it would live *inside* —
made of the same stuff as the numbers it commands, loaded and swapped like thread.
Software, before anyone called it that.

The machines in this story were all contested, superseded, dismantled. The pattern
was not.

## ENDING

So what is this story about?

Not the loom — looms wear out. Not the Analytical Engine — it never existed. Not
the tubes — they burned out by the day. Every machine in this story died. The thing
that survived is the thing none of them could hold: the pattern, separated from the
mechanism — a decision, hole or no hole, that can be copied, carried, and hung on
whatever machine comes next.

Your phone becomes a thousand different machines a day — a map, a piano, a
darkroom. Same glass, same silicon. Different cards on the loom.

And right now, somewhere, someone is writing a pattern for a machine that does not
exist yet.

That is not a mistake. That is how this story has always started.

## Description-block notes (accuracy, off-narration)

For the video description or a pinned comment — true, but cut from the voiceover to
protect the spine:

- "First programmer" is contested: Babbage left earlier unpublished sketches; some
  historians read Note G as an execution trace. Careful claim used on-screen:
  first *published* algorithm for a general-purpose machine.
- ENIAC's precise title: first *general-purpose, programmable, electronic* digital
  computer. Zuse's Z3 (1941) was programmable but electromechanical; the
  Atanasoff–Berry Computer was electronic but not programmable; Colossus (1944) was
  electronic and programmable but special-purpose and secret until the 1970s. The
  ENIAC patent was voided in *Honeywell v. Sperry Rand* (1973).
- The 1945 stored-program report ("First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC")
  circulated under von Neumann's name alone; Eckert and Mauchly's credit remains
  disputed.
- Hollerith timing stated carefully: 1890 census tabulated in a fraction of the
  prior census's time, results roughly two years earlier; savings commonly put at
  ~$5M. Tabulating Machine Co. (1896) → CTR (1911) → renamed IBM (1924).
- Jacquard riot specifics vary by source — narration says "by the accounts."
