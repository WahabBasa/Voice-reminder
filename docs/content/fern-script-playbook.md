# Fern script playbook

## THE LOCKED ANATOMY (final formula, settled 2026-08-22 after 8 iterations)

Every historical-narrative script beat gets all six organs, in this order. This
supersedes everything below where they conflict; the sections below are the raw
lessons this was distilled from.

1. **World first.** Build the place and its economy before any person appears. Every
   later "coincidence" must be a consequence of the world (silk money buys music
   machines → the two trades share streets). Coincidence upstream of the world =
   authorial cheating; downstream = inevitability.
2. **The priced cage.** The protagonist's motive is never curiosity — it's arithmetic
   with a number (Damian's 100 Vigha; the weaver's piecework: ruined silk comes out
   of HIS bread). State the cage before any invention begins.
3. **The inevitability inversion.** Never "he happened to have X." Instead: the world
   made the crossing certain; the person is just who it landed on. Template line:
   "The one it happened to was named ___."
4. **The viewer's click.** Lay the two ingredients as paired images ("the loom, which
   cannot remember its own pattern — the organ, which never forgets its song") and
   DELETE the conceptual bridge. The viewer makes the connection; the character's
   action confirms it. Never write "he realized."
5. **The study montage.** Invention is a process, never an act. Frame honestly once
   ("Nobody recorded his experiments. But we know the machine he finished with — and
   a finished machine is a list of problems its builder had to solve"), then:
   problem → attempt → visible failure receipt (garbled strip, sheared figure) →
   forced fix → success receipt (correct, correct, correct). Two problems max per
   montage. ONE causal link per beat — secondary links die, they don't get
   explained faster.
6. **The receipt that hands the baton.** End each invention on the failure mode that
   forces the next inventor (paper tears → Falcon's cards).

## The skeleton (in order)

1. **Cold open on the object.** "This is a ___." One sentence naming the thing + its
   function. No throat-clearing, no "have you ever wondered."
2. **Scale in numbers, instantly.** Two sentences in: "over 200 caught... many more out
   there." Stakes arrive as a count, not an adjective.
3. **The menu contract.** "Today we look at four models, from simple to transatlantic
   high-tech" + three explicit questions the video promises to answer. The viewer knows
   the shape of the meal before the first course.
4. **One context block** (the game being played: smuggling economics, cat-and-mouse) —
   short, then never returned to as exposition again.
5. **A mythologized maker.** The story anchors on the *builder* (the "king of
   semi-submersibles," hidden jungle shipyard), not the product's users. Villain or
   hero, the craftsman is the protagonist.
6. **A real expert as recurring voice.** Credentialed, first-person, texture-bearing
   ("I've been looking at these for 15 years, keeping a database"). Narrator states;
   expert confirms with lived detail. Alternating rhythm.
7. **Case studies on an escalation ladder.** Each unit: specs (exact numbers) →
   interior tour (sensory, cramped, human) → a narrated incident (present tense) →
   the inference/lesson. Each case tops the last on one axis.
8. **The inversion near the top.** The "peak" specimen subverts the ladder (the best
   sub is the *smallest and slowest* — towed, electric). Escalation ends in a twist,
   not a bigger number.
9. **Cyclical non-ending.** The king is arrested BUT "a new genius is already working
   on the next generation." Resolution refused; the system continues. Forward-looking
   last line.

## The devices

- **Present-tense live simulation.** "Right now, five men sit in tense silence." A
  typical/composite journey narrated as if watched live. This is the engine of
  immersion — the script *rides along*, it doesn't recount.
- **Receipts as texture.** Numbers in nearly every paragraph: 14 m, 16 km/h, 7.7 tons,
  20,000 L, 6,500 km, $1.5M, 10 tons of batteries, 12 hours endurance. Every claim
  priced. (Maps 1:1 to the user's ledger taste.)
- **Deixis for the animation.** "Do you see this little thing here? That's a towing
  ring." The script points at the frame — written for diagrams, not against them.
- **One-clause humans.** "August Tejón Alvarez, a former Spanish boxer and captain of
  the vessel." Name + single vivid credential. Suffering rendered in objects: plastic
  bags as toilets, cans of sardines, one proper seat.
- **Negative-space inference as drama.** "Not one has ever been caught at sea — but we
  keep finding them on land, so they must be very successful." Reasoning from absence,
  spoken aloud, is a suspense beat.
- **Permission to admire.** One line handles the moral distance ("obviously organized
  crime... but the ingenuity is undeniably remarkable") and frees the viewer to enjoy
  the engineering.
- **The doomed specific human arc.** One case study carries a full human story with a
  ticking constraint (27 days, fuel gone, two rendezvous failures, five days hiding in
  an abandoned house). Exactly one — not every case.
- **Ad bridge via theme.** The sponsor segue rides the video's own thesis ("if you
  want to build these skills yourself...").
- **Craftsman signature theory.** "Each designer has a signature... certain ways of
  solving problems that reveal themselves in the designs." Attribution-by-style as a
  recurring analytical lens.

## Sentence rhythm

Short declaratives dominate. Specs delivered flat, then one sensory line ("the low hum
of the diesel engine pulses through the hull"). No rhetorical questions after the
opening menu. Verbs carry the drama; adjectives are rationed.

## The essayist school (second reference: the pay-to-win history script, 2026-08-21)

A different engine from Fern's — additive, not competing. Its devices:

- **Time-travel sensory anchors.** One line of smell/sound/light per location and the
  viewer is *there*: "the aroma of heated circuitry and musty carpet." Every era
  change gets one.
- **Anachronistic bridges.** Explain the old thing through what the viewer already
  knows: shareware = "like a phone game — first levels free, rest paywalled." The
  familiar-to-magical weave lives in these bridges. At least one per act.
- **Causal tissue.** Every step has a *because*, and the because has a number:
  arcades wanted you to fail because cabinets cost $10k; Doom went free because
  publishers took the royalties. Never "then X happened" — always "X had to happen,
  because."
- **Light words.** High word count, low fact density: one simple image per sentence,
  a playback video in the mind. Spend three sentences building one picture; cut a
  fact to afford it. Overwhelm is a fact problem, not a length problem.
- **Personal-anecdote slots.** First-person memories ("I remember a swap meet in
  2005...") — leave marked slots for the creator's own; never fabricate them.
- **Inline concept teaching.** A term the story needs (price elasticity) gets one
  conversational breath of explanation right where it's needed, never a sidebar.

## The Johnny Harris interleave (added after v5 user test, 2026-08-21)

Story leads; explanation follows in bites. SHOW (a scene moves, someone does
something) → one small context bite (1–2 sentences, never a standalone explainer
block) → SHOW again → bite again. If a concept needs more than two sentences, split
it across scenes — teach a piece exactly where the scene needs it, never before.
Symptoms of violation: any paragraph that starts "To understand X, you need to
know…", or two consecutive paragraphs with no person doing anything. v5's act one
failed exactly this way: a two-paragraph weaving lesson before the story moved.

## Write for the ear, not the eye (added after v1/v2 user tests, 2026-08-21)

A line that shines on the page can be a riddle at speaking speed. Rules learned the
hard way:

- **Explain first, epigram after.** "The pattern isn't written down anywhere — it
  exists only in the weaver's memory" BEFORE any compressed aphorism. v1's "stored in
  the only place it can be stored: a person" confused a listener instantly.
- **One idea per sentence.** Em-dash appositives and stacked clauses that read fine
  silently overload the ear.
- **Ground every list item.** A menu of three things needs a concrete tag each ("a
  loom in France, that weaves silk") — bare abstractions ("one idea trying to escape
  them") give the listener nothing to hold.
- **Don't engineer pauses.** TTS paces itself off punctuation; injected breaks and
  rate changes read as awkward stops. Write the rhythm into the sentences instead.
- **The test is TTS, not reading.** Render a section and listen before calling it
  done; the ear finds what the eye forgives.

## Mapping template for the loom→ENIAC script

- Cold open: "This is a punched card." (object first)
- Menu: three machines — a loom, an engine never built, a 30-ton room
- Makers as protagonists: Jacquard (riots, Napoleon), Babbage + Lovelace (the woven
  portrait he owned), the six ENIAC women (no manuals — learned it from wiring diagrams)
- Receipts throughout: card counts, tube counts, setup-days vs computation-seconds
- The inversion: the machine's power was never the machine — the cards were the point
  (pattern separated from mechanism = the intangible "utility")
- Non-ending: the same ghost now lives in everything with a chip
